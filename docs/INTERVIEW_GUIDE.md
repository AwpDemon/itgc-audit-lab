# Interview Guide - itgc-audit-lab

This guide is for me (Ali) to prepare for Big-4 technology-risk / IT-audit / IT-rotation interviews.
It explains the subject from scratch, then every design decision in this repo, then the questions
I am likely to get and how to answer them honestly. Every number quoted here comes from
`audit/results/fy26-baseline/results.json` or the remediation re-test, and can be regenerated with
`python -m itgc audit`.

---

## 0. The 60-second version

> "I wanted to learn what a tech-risk associate actually does, so I built a small company's IT
> environment and audited it. I stood up an Active Directory domain controller (Samba 4) and an ERP
> database in Docker, seeded them with a fictional 81-person company that had a year of hires,
> transfers, leavers, changes and job runs, and planted 39 issues. Then I ran the engagement:
> scoping memo, a 15-control risk and control matrix mapped to COBIT, NIST 800-53 and SOC 2,
> Python and PowerShell scripts that pull evidence read-only over LDAPS and SQL and hash it,
> 100% data analytics where I could and frequency-based sampling where I could not, Excel
> workpapers generated by code, a deficiency evaluation, and a management letter. 12 of 15
> controls failed, 8 as significant deficiencies; the tests found all 39 planted issues. The most
> useful lesson was sampling risk: my random sample of 15 changes said the change control was
> effective, and the same test over all 32 changes found 4 exceptions. Then I remediated the lab
> and re-tested: 43 exceptions went to 20, and what was left were things you cannot fix
> retroactively, like a change that was deployed without approval."

Pause there; let them pick what to dig into.

## 1. ITGCs from scratch

### What a control is
A **control** is a routine that prevents or detects something going wrong. "The CFO approves
payments over $10k" is a control. "Only the Release Manager can deploy to production" is a control.

### Application controls vs ITGCs
* **Application (automated) controls** live inside a business system: the ERP refuses to post an
  unbalanced journal entry; it blocks an order over the customer's credit limit; it does a
  three-way match before paying an invoice.
* **IT general controls (ITGCs)** are the controls *around* the systems that make those automated
  controls trustworthy. If anyone can change the ERP code, the credit-limit check might have been
  switched off. If a fired employee can still log in, "only the AP manager approves payments"
  means nothing.

So ITGCs are **foundational**: the financial-statement auditor can only rely on automated controls
and system-generated reports if ITGCs operated effectively for the whole period.

### The ITGC domains (the four boxes every firm uses)
1. **Access to programs and data** (logical access): provisioning, de-provisioning, privileged
   access, periodic access reviews, password/authentication settings. *My AC-01 to AC-07.*
2. **Program changes** (change management): changes are authorized, tested, approved, and
   developers cannot push their own code to production (segregation of duties). *CM-01 to CM-04.*
3. **Computer operations**: backups, restores, batch job monitoring, incident/problem management.
   *OPS-01 to OPS-04.*
4. **Program development**: new systems and big implementations (data conversion, go-live
   approval). *Out of scope here because the company implemented no new system in FY2026; I say
   so in the scoping memo.*

### Where ITGCs sit in the bigger picture
* **Entity-level controls** (tone at the top, policies, risk assessment) - COSO's control
  environment.
* **ITGCs** - support everything technical.
* **Business process / application controls** - the controls in order-to-cash, procure-to-pay,
  financial close.
* **IPE / key reports** (information produced by the entity) - reports the auditor uses, e.g. the
  AP aging. ITGCs plus a completeness-and-accuracy test make IPE reliable.

## 2. SOX 404 in plain English

* **Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002)**, after Enron/WorldCom. Section **302**: CEO/CFO certify the
  financial reports. Section **404(a)**: management must assess and report on the effectiveness of
  internal control over financial reporting (ICFR). Section **404(b)**: for accelerated filers, the
  external auditor must attest to that assessment (an "integrated audit").
* **Framework**: almost everyone uses **COSO 2013** for ICFR. IT-specific detail comes from
  **COBIT**. The PCAOB auditing standard for the integrated audit is **AS 2201**.
* **Top-down, risk-based approach** (AS 2201): start with the financial statements and significant
  accounts, identify what could go wrong, find the **key controls** that address those risks, then
  find the IT systems those controls depend on. Those systems are the ITGC scope. That is what my
  scoping memo does in miniature.
* **Design effectiveness vs operating effectiveness**:
  * *Design* - if the control operates as described, would it address the risk? Tested with a
    **walkthrough** (follow one transaction end to end; inquiry + inspection + observation).
  * *Operating* - did it actually operate as designed throughout the period? Tested with samples
    or analytics over the period. My tests are tests of operating effectiveness.
* **Test of one**: an *automated* control (e.g. the domain password policy) behaves the same way
  every time, so testing it once is enough, *provided* ITGCs over changes to it are effective. If
  change management fails, you cannot rely on "it was configured right when I looked" - that is
  exactly the link between AC-05 and the change domain.
* **Interim vs year-end and roll-forward**: auditors often test at interim (e.g. 9 months) and
  then "roll forward" with inquiry and limited testing for the rest of the year.
* **Who does what in a Big-4 tech-risk team**: associates test ITGCs and IPE, document
  workpapers, draft findings; seniors review and run client meetings; managers make severity
  calls. My lab plays the associate, and the rule set in `deficiency.py` is my attempt to make the
  manager's judgement explicit.

## 3. Evidence: types, quality, and how I collected it

### The four procedure types (weakest to strongest)
1. **Inquiry** - asking someone. Never sufficient on its own.
2. **Observation** - watching someone do it. Only proves it happened at that moment.
3. **Inspection** - examining documents/records (a ticket, a sign-off, a config export).
4. **Re-performance** - the auditor independently does it again (recomputes, re-matches, restores
   the backup). Strongest.

In my workpapers every procedure is labelled with its type. Inquiry steps are documented but marked
*not performed* because there is nobody to interview in a lab - and I say so rather than pretend.

### Sufficient and appropriate evidence
* *Sufficient* = enough of it (sample size). *Appropriate* = relevant and reliable.
* Reliability ranking: evidence obtained **directly by the auditor from the system** beats
  evidence handed over by the client; original beats copy; documented beats verbal.
* That is why my collectors connect to the systems **myself**, with **read-only accounts**
  (`svc_audit_ro` in AD, `audit_ro` with `pg_read_all_data` in PostgreSQL), instead of asking IT for
  a spreadsheet. A screenshot from IT could be filtered; an LDAP query I run cannot.

### IPE - completeness and accuracy of populations
Before sampling, you must know the population is complete (nothing missing) and accurate. My
examples:
* CI/CD deployment log reconciled to git tags: 32 deployments = 32 tags, no orphans either way
  (`completeness` block in CM-01/CM-02).
* AD accounts created in the period reconciled to HR hires (no unexpected accounts).
* Backup log has one run for each of the 365 days.

### Evidence integrity
Every evidence file gets a SHA-256 hash in `manifest.json`; `itgc audit` refuses to run if a hash
does not match, and CI re-verifies. In interview terms: "my workpapers can be traced to exactly the
evidence collected, and nobody - including me - can quietly edit it afterwards."

### PBC
"Provided by client" - the request list. My PBC items (HR roster, ticket exports, review sign-offs,
job logs, the security standard) are in `audit/pbc/` and listed in the `PBC` sheet.

## 4. Sampling - how sizes are chosen and why my sample "lied"

### Frequency-based sample sizes
ITGC testing usually uses a table keyed on how often the control operates:

| Frequency | Typical sample |
|---|---|
| Annual | 1 |
| Quarterly | 2 |
| Monthly | 2-5 |
| Weekly | 5-15 |
| Daily | 20-40 |
| Multiple times a day | 25-60 |

Low end = lower risk or other evidence available; high end = higher risk / sole mitigating control.
Exact numbers differ by firm - say "firm methodologies vary, but they look like this."
**Event-driven** populations (every new hire, every change) are mapped to the band with a similar
annual count: 32 changes is roughly weekly, high risk, so n = 15.

### Zero expected deviations
ITGC samples are designed assuming **zero** deviations. One exception means the control did not
operate effectively (some methodologies let you extend the sample once if the deviation looks
isolated; I treat the first one as a failure and say so).

### Selection
**Random** selection, reproducible from a seed (`FY2026:<control id>`), so a reviewer can re-draw
exactly my sample. Alternatives: haphazard (no conscious bias, but not random), systematic (every
k-th item), and targeted/judgmental (e.g. all failed backups).

### Sampling risk, with real numbers
The chance that a random sample of `n` from `N` items with `k` bad ones contains at least one bad
item is the hypergeometric probability `1 - C(N-k, n) / C(N, n)`.
* CM-01: N = 32, k = 4, n = 15 -> **93.4%** chance of catching one. My seeded draw landed in the
  other 6.6%: the sample contained none of them.
* Backups: N = 365 days, k = 2 unticketed failures, n = 20 -> only **10.7%**. To get to 95% you
  would need over 200 days - which is why you *target* the failures instead of sampling days.

What I did about it: for sampled controls I also ran the same attribute tests over 100% of the
population (it is all machine-readable), and **deviations found outside the sample still have to be
evaluated** - you cannot un-know them. So CM-01 is reported as "sample: effective; final: not
effective, 4 exceptions identified by full-population analytics." Across the sampled controls,
samples found 2 of 8 planted exceptions; analytics found 8 of 8.

The interview line: *"Sampling is a cost decision, not a truth machine. When the whole population
is in a database, test all of it."*

## 5. Deficiency evaluation

### Definitions (AS 2201 Appendix A; same idea in AU-C 265 / ISA 265)
* **Control deficiency (CD)** - the design or operation of a control does not allow management or
  employees to prevent or detect misstatements on a timely basis.
* **Significant deficiency (SD)** - less severe than a material weakness, yet important enough to
  merit the attention of those charged with governance (the audit committee).
* **Material weakness (MW)** - a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement will not be
  prevented or detected on a timely basis. One MW means management cannot conclude ICFR is effective
  (an adverse 404(b) opinion).

### How severity is judged
Two dimensions: **likelihood** (could it lead to a misstatement - and did it?) and **magnitude**
(how big could it be?), adjusted for **compensating controls** and evaluated **in aggregate**.
ITGC deficiencies do not misstate anything directly; you ask "which automated controls and reports
depended on this ITGC, and is there a business-process control that would catch the error anyway?"

### My rule set (`src/itgc/deficiency.py`)
1. Any exception -> control not effective -> at least a CD.
2. Escalate to SD when the system is financially significant **and** (the risk was *realised* in the
   evidence - e.g. a post-termination logon, an unapproved change actually deployed - **or** the
   weakness is *pervasive* - domain-wide policy, tier-0 or database-admin access) **and** there is no
   *effective* compensating control.
3. Aggregate by risk: if two or more controls on the same risk failed, evaluate the combination; if
   no control on that risk is left effective, it is an SD.
4. Realised **and** pervasive -> flag for MW consideration by the engagement team (I do not
   conclude MW: that needs the financial-statement context I do not have).

### Worked examples from my results
* **AC-03 (privileged access)**: a developer is a Domain Admin and a database superuser; the backup
  service account is a Domain Admin. Realised (the access exists today) and pervasive (tier 0) ->
  SD, flagged for MW consideration.
* **AC-02 (leavers)**: one leaver logged on 6 days after termination. The compensating control would
  be the quarterly access review (AC-04) - but AC-04 also failed, so it cannot compensate -> SD.
* **AC-06 (dormant accounts)**: 6 dormant accounts but no evidence of misuse and not pervasive -> CD.
  It still contributes to the aggregated SD on risk R-APD-3 (former employees / unused accounts),
  because every control on that risk failed.
* **AC-05 after remediation**: once the domain policy was fixed, the remaining issue was a single
  service account with "password never expires" -> not pervasive -> drops to CD.

### Writing it up: condition / criteria / cause / effect / recommendation
Every finding in the management letter has the five parts: what we found (**condition**), what
should be (**criteria** - the client's own ISS-04 standard), why it happened (**cause** - written as
"likely, to be confirmed with management"), so what (**effect/risk**), and what to do
(**recommendation**). Management responses are left as placeholders; in a real engagement you get
them before issuing.

## 6. Active Directory from scratch

* **Domain**: a security boundary of users, computers and groups (`kestrel.lab.test`).
  **Domain controller (DC)**: the server holding the directory database and authenticating
  logins. **Forest**: one or more domains with a shared schema.
* **Protocols**: **LDAP** (port 389) to read/write the directory; **LDAPS** (636) is LDAP over TLS;
  **Kerberos** (88) for authentication tickets; DNS to find DCs. My collector uses LDAPS and
  validates the DC's certificate against its CA.
* **Objects**: users, groups, computers, OUs. A **DN** (distinguished name) is the path:
  `CN=klee,OU=Users,OU=Kestrel,DC=kestrel,DC=lab,DC=test`.
* **OU vs group**: an **OU** is a folder for *administration and Group Policy*; a **group** is for
  *permissions*. Security groups grant access; distribution groups are mail lists. Groups can be
  **nested** - which is why I query recursive membership with the
  `LDAP_MATCHING_RULE_IN_CHAIN` OID (`1.2.840.113556.1.4.1941`): a user in a group that is inside
  Domain Admins is a Domain Admin.
* **Privileged ("protected") groups**: Domain Admins, Enterprise Admins, Schema Admins,
  Administrators, Account Operators, Backup Operators, Server Operators, Print Operators, Group
  Policy Creator Owners, DnsAdmins. Backup Operators can read every file on a DC (including the AD
  database), so it is effectively tier 0 - that is why `svc_backup` there still counts after
  remediation.
* **userAccountControl** - a bit field: 0x2 disabled, 0x20 password not required, 0x200 normal
  account, 0x10000 password never expires, 0x400000 no Kerberos pre-auth. `decode_uac()` turns
  `66050` into `ACCOUNTDISABLE, NORMAL_ACCOUNT, DONT_EXPIRE_PASSWORD`.
* **Logon timestamps**: `lastLogon` is per-DC and not replicated; `lastLogonTimestamp` is
  replicated but only updated when it is 9-14 days stale (so use a 14-day tolerance);
  PowerShell's `LastLogonDate` is just `lastLogonTimestamp` converted. Timestamps are **FILETIME**:
  100-nanosecond ticks since 1 Jan 1601 UTC.
* **Password policy**: the default domain policy (on the domain object: `minPwdLength`,
  `lockoutThreshold`, `maxPwdAge` ...) applies to everyone; **fine-grained password policies
  (PSOs)** override it for specific groups (I gave service accounts a 20-character PSO). By default
  only admins can read PSOs - my read-only account could not, which I record as a scope limitation.
* **Service accounts** run applications; best practice is **gMSA** (group managed service accounts,
  automatic password rotation) and "deny interactive logon". **krbtgt** is the Kerberos key
  account (disabled by design; its password should be rotated periodically).
* **Joiner / mover / leaver (JML)**: the identity lifecycle. Joiner = provision from an approved
  request; mover = add new access *and remove old* (privilege creep is the classic finding);
  leaver = disable on the last day.
* **PowerShell equivalents** on a domain-joined machine with RSAT:
  `Get-ADUser -Filter * -Properties lastLogonTimestamp,whenCreated,userAccountControl,memberOf`,
  `Get-ADGroupMember 'Domain Admins' -Recursive`, `Get-ADDefaultDomainPasswordPolicy`,
  `Search-ADAccount -AccountInactive -TimeSpan 90.00:00:00`. My `Get-ADEvidence.ps1` does not need
  RSAT (it uses `System.DirectoryServices.Protocols` with signing and sealing), which is why it also
  works against Samba.
* **Samba vs Windows Server**: Samba 4 implements the AD DC protocols (LDAP, Kerberos, the same
  schema and attributes), so the LDAP queries are identical. It does not run AD Web Services (so no
  RSAT `Get-AD*` cmdlets against it) and I did not build Group Policy objects. I chose Samba because
  the Windows Server evaluation requires an account sign-up, and Samba builds from Debian packages
  in a reproducible container.

## 8. Design decisions (and why)

* **Build the systems instead of faking evidence.** A JSON file of "users" proves nothing; pulling
  evidence over LDAPS from a real DC with a read-only account is the actual skill.
* **Samba in Docker, on a second PC.** No Microsoft account needed; reproducible; it ran on my
  second machine so my main server stayed free. Ports bind to 127.0.0.1 and I reached them over an
  SSH tunnel - the lab was never on the network.
* **Back-dated timestamps.** A fresh directory has no history, so the seeder (which has root on the
  DC) back-dates `whenCreated` and `lastLogonTimestamp` with the LDB relax control. `pwdLastSet`
  cannot be back-dated, so password age is out of scope. This is documented, not hidden.
* **Answer key the tests never read.** Planting issues and then scoring detection is how you test
  an audit program. 4 exceptions were not on the key; all are knock-on effects (e.g. a leaver who
  is also a dormant account). An early run also flagged two disabled leavers as logging on after
  termination - that turned out to be a bug in my *generator* (a shift-worker logon override applied
  to terminated people), which I fixed. Good story: the audit tests caught a data problem.
* **Three controls deliberately clean.** A lab where everything fails is not realistic and would
  not exercise the "effective" path.
* **Analytics first, sampling where needed.** It mirrors where the profession is going and it let
  me measure sampling risk instead of just describing it.
* **Hashes on evidence; CI re-runs the audit.** Reproducibility is an audit concept: a reviewer must
  be able to re-perform my work from my evidence.
* **Rules for severity in code.** Severity is judgement; writing the rules down makes the judgement
  reviewable and consistent. A manager could disagree with a rule, but not with how it was applied.
* **Python + PowerShell collectors with a parity test.** Many clients are Windows shops; showing the
  same evidence two ways, and proving they agree field by field, is a credibility check.
* **Excel workpapers generated from code.** Auditors live in Excel; the code just removes
  transcription errors. Each control sheet has the objective, procedures, attributes, tickmarks
  (check / X / n/a), detail per item, and a conclusion.

## 9. Likely interview questions - and strong answers

**"Walk me through how you would test user de-provisioning."**
Get the population of terminations from HR (the system of record, not IT). Check its completeness
(e.g. reconcile to payroll). For each leaver - or all of them, if I can get system data - check the AD
account is disabled, the application account is inactive, and the timing against the policy
(1 business day here), using ticket closure or, in Windows, security event 4725 / replication
metadata for when the account was actually disabled. Also check `lastLogonTimestamp` against the
termination date: a logon after leaving is the worst case. In my lab that found two leavers still
enabled, one of whom logged on six days after termination.

**"What is the difference between design and operating effectiveness?"**
Design: would the control address the risk if it worked as described (walkthrough). Operating: did
it work throughout the period (sample or analytics). Example: an access-review control can be well
designed and still fail operating effectiveness because Q2 was signed 41 days late.

**"You found one exception in a sample of 25. Is the control ineffective?"**
ITGC samples assume zero deviations, so by default yes. Some methodologies allow extending the
sample once if the deviation looks isolated and explainable, but you document the root cause either
way, and you consider whether it changes reliance on the automated controls downstream.

**"How do you pick a sample size?"**
By control frequency and risk: annual 1, quarterly 2, monthly 2-5, weekly 5-15, daily 20-40. An
event-driven population is mapped to the band with a similar count. Then random selection with a
recorded seed. And if the population is in a database, consider testing 100%.

**"Your sample found nothing, but your analytics found four exceptions. Which do you report?"**
Both, and the conclusion is "not effective". Once you know about deviations outside the sample, you
must evaluate them. The sample-only result is useful as a demonstration of sampling risk (6.6% chance
of that miss here), not as a conclusion.

**"What makes evidence reliable?"**
Obtained directly from the system by the auditor, complete and accurate population (IPE tested),
original rather than copies, and tamper-evident. I pulled from the systems with read-only accounts and
hashed every file.

**"What is IPE and how do you test it?"**
Information produced by the entity - any report or extract you rely on. Test completeness (nothing
missing: reconcile to an independent source or record count) and accuracy (fields are right: agree a
few records back to the source, or understand and test the query). Example: I reconciled 32 CI/CD
deployments to 32 git tags before sampling changes.

**"How would you classify a developer with Domain Admin rights?"**
Not effective privileged-access control. Severity depends on whether the access was used and on
compensating controls. In my lab the same developer also deployed their own code and applied a database
change outside the release process, so the risk was realised, it is pervasive (tier 0), and the change
controls that could have compensated also failed: significant deficiency, flagged for material-weakness
consideration.

**"What is segregation of duties and how do you test it in change management?"**
The person who writes a change should not be able to approve or deploy it. Test: compare the commit
authors of each release with the deployer, and check the deployer is in the authorized deploy group.
I did that for all 32 releases: 3 were self-deployed.

**"What is the difference between a significant deficiency and a material weakness?"**
Both are communicated to the audit committee. A material weakness means a reasonable possibility of a
material misstatement not being prevented or detected; it makes ICFR ineffective. An SD is less severe
but still merits governance attention. ITGC deficiencies are evaluated through their impact on the
automated controls and reports that depend on them.

**"How do ITGC failures affect the financial audit?"**
If ITGCs over a system fail, the auditor cannot rely on that system's automated controls or reports
without more work: test the automated controls at more points in time, test compensating manual
controls, or do more substantive testing. That costs hours, so clients care.

**"What would you do differently in a real engagement?"**
Hold the walkthroughs (inquiry and observation with control owners), obtain management responses,
use real change history (security logs, replication metadata) for timing, agree severity with the
senior/manager, and get the PBC list out early.

**"Explain lastLogon vs lastLogonTimestamp."**
`lastLogon` is per-DC and not replicated - you would need to query every DC and take the max.
`lastLogonTimestamp` is replicated but only updated when it is 9-14 days old, so it is fine for
"inactive 90 days" tests with a tolerance, and a value *after* a termination date is conclusive.

**"How did you map to frameworks?"**
Each control has COBIT 2019 practices (e.g. DSS05.04 identity and logical access, BAI06.01 change
authorization, DSS04.07 backups), NIST 800-53 controls (AC-2, AC-2(3), AC-5, AC-6(5), IA-5(1), CM-3,
CM-5, CP-9) and SOC 2 criteria (CC6.x for access, CC8.1 for change, A1.2 for backup). They are my
judgement of the closest objective - mapping is not one-to-one.

**"Why Samba and not Windows Server?"**
Same protocols and attributes, so the evidence queries are identical; reproducible in a container; no
sign-up. Trade-off: no Group Policy objects and no RSAT cmdlets, which I list as next steps.

**"What about a one-person environment like your homelab - how do you do SoD?"**
You cannot. Be honest about it and use a detective compensating control: everything in git, and a
periodic review of what is published and who can reach it. That is what my `home/` section does.

**"Tell me about a time something did not work."**
My first run flagged two disabled leavers as logging on months after termination. Instead of trusting
it, I traced it: my data generator gave shift workers a recent logon and applied it to terminated
ones too. I fixed the generator, re-seeded the lab from scratch, and re-collected. The audit tests
were right; my "client data" was wrong - which is also why you test IPE.

## 10. Limitations - what to say when asked

* "The company and its tickets are synthetic; the systems and the evidence collection are real."
* "AD has no history of group membership or disable times; I used ticket closure as the timing
  evidence and current membership as a proxy. In Windows I would use event logs or replication
  metadata."
* "I could not interview anyone, so inquiry and observation steps are documented but not performed."
* "Severity is judgement; I wrote down my rules so they can be challenged."
* "The restore timing is from a 44 KB database, so it proves the method, not a recovery-time
  objective."
* "Sampling results come from one seeded draw; the probability curve shows the expected behaviour."

## 11. Glossary

* **Attribute** - one thing checked per sample item (A: ticket exists, B: approved before deploy ...).
* **CAB** - change advisory board, approves changes.
* **Compensating control** - another control that addresses the same risk if the primary one fails.
* **Exception / deviation** - an item where an attribute failed.
* **gMSA** - group managed service account (AD rotates its password).
* **ICFR** - internal control over financial reporting.
* **IPE** - information produced by the entity (reports, extracts).
* **JML** - joiner / mover / leaver.
* **Key control** - a control the auditor relies on to address a risk.
* **PBC** - provided by client.
* **PSO** - password settings object (fine-grained password policy).
* **RCM** - risk and control matrix.
